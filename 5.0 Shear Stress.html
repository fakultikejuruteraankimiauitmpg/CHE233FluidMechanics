1 What is Shear Stress in a Fluid?

When a fluid flows, different layers of the fluid may move at different velocities.

When these fluid layers move relative to one another, a shear stress develops.

Simulation · Fluid Between Two Plates Top plate drags the fluid — layers slide past each other
What you are seeing

This is Couette flow — the most basic flow in fluid mechanics. The top plate moves to the right at speed U, while the bottom plate is fixed.

  • Blue arrows = the velocity of each fluid layer. The topmost arrow is long (high speed), the bottommost is short (low speed).
  • White particles = fluid points drifting with their respective layers. Particles at the top travel further in the same time.
  • Moving blue plate = the surface dragging the fluid. Fixed grey plate = the surface holding the fluid back.
Watch this: as you increase the slider U, the top arrow grows longer, but the bottom arrow stays near zero. The gap between the top and bottom arrow lengths is the velocity gradient (du/dy) — and that is where shear stress exists.

Why it matters: this is why a fluid "sticks" to a solid surface. The layer touching the moving plate moves at the same speed as the plate (no-slip condition), and each subsequent layer is dragged along by the one below it — producing the gradient.

Imagine water flowing between two plates: the fluid layer close to the moving plate moves faster, while the fluid layer close to the fixed plate moves more slowly.

Therefore, there is a difference in velocity between fluid layers. This difference produces a velocity gradient — and that gradient is what generates shear stress.

Key idea: shear stress in a fluid is not caused by the fluid "moving" — it is caused by neighbouring layers moving at different velocities.

2 Real-Life Example 1 · Water in a River

Think about water flowing along a river.

The water near the surface can move faster, while the water near the river bed moves more slowly because of interaction with the river bed.

Simulation · River Velocity Profile Surface fast, bed slow — layers slide over each other
What you are seeing

This is a river cross-section. The water surface (wavy line above) and the river bed (brown block below) sandwich the water in between.

  • FAST at the surface — surface water particles do not rub against any solid surface, so they are most free to move.
  • MEDIUM in the middle — this layer is dragged by the layer above but held back by the layer below.
  • SLOW near the bed — friction with the river bed (no-slip) reduces the speed almost to zero.
Watch this: the arrows are not equal in length. Each layer moves at a different speed → there is a velocity gradient inside the water → there is shear stress inside the water. This is what causes water to swirl and mix.

Why it matters: engineers use this velocity profile to calculate discharge (flow rate), kinetic energy, and the drag force on the river bed.

Surface FAST Middle MEDIUM Near bed SLOW

Because adjacent layers travel at different speeds, the river has an internal velocity gradient — and therefore internal shear stress.

Different velocities → Velocity gradient → Shear stress The same chain appears in every real flow

3 Real-Life Example 2 · Water in a Pipe

When water flows through a pipe, the fluid close to the pipe wall moves more slowly, while the fluid near the centre of the pipe moves faster.

Simulation · Pipe Cross-Section The velocity profile is fastest at the centre
What you are seeing

A pipe cross-section. The pipe walls are above and below (grey blocks), and the fluid is in the middle (blue).

  • The longest arrow sits at the pipe centre — this is the maximum velocity (centre-line velocity).
  • The shortest arrow sits at the wall — nearly zero because of the no-slip condition: fluid touching a solid wall does not move relative to it.
  • The shape of this velocity curve is called a parabolic velocity profile for laminar flow (Hagen–Poiseuille).
Watch this: if you increase the centre speed slider, the parabola becomes "sharper" but the wall velocity stays at zero. The difference between the centre speed and wall speed is what determines the wall shear stress acting on the pipe.

Why it matters: wall shear stress is what causes energy loss (head loss) in pipes, and it determines how much pump power is needed to maintain the flow.

Because the fluid sticks to the wall (the no-slip condition), velocity must rise from zero at the wall to a maximum at the centre. Adjacent fluid layers therefore have different velocities.

4 Real-Life Example 3 · Pouring Honey

When honey is poured, different layers of honey move relative to each other as the honey flows.

Because honey has a relatively high viscosity, it strongly resists this relative motion. Therefore, shear stress is developed within the flowing honey.

Simulation · Same Shear Stress, Different Fluids Same driving force → water shears fast, honey shears slowly
What you are seeing

Two fluid cells given the same shear stress (same driving force):

  • LEFT — WATER: low μ. For the same τ, water produces a large velocity gradient → long arrows → flows fast.
  • RIGHT — HONEY: high μ. For the same τ, honey produces only a small velocity gradient → short arrows → flows slowly.
Watch this: even though τ is the same, the arrow lengths are very different between the two cells. This is the meaning of τ = μ · du/dy: when μ is large, du/dy must be small for τ to remain the same — and vice versa.

Why it matters: this is why honey can "stand" on bread without flowing down, while water keeps flowing. High viscosity means the fluid strongly resists relative motion between its layers.

Water → low viscosity → large velocity gradient for the same shear stress.

Honey → high viscosity → small velocity gradient for the same shear stress.

Both fluids experience shear stress — but honey resists the deformation far more strongly.

5 What is Velocity Gradient?

The velocity gradient describes how rapidly the fluid velocity changes with distance perpendicular to the flow.

du / dy u = fluid velocity · y = distance perpendicular to the direction of flow

In simple words: velocity gradient = how quickly the velocity changes from one fluid layer to another.

Simulation · Two Layers, One Gradient Change the two layer velocities and the gap between them
Δu = u₂ − u₁
1.40 m/s
Velocity gradient du/dy
350 s⁻¹
What you are seeing

Two fluid layers: one on top (speed u₂), one below (speed u₁), separated by distance Δy.

  • Δu (orange arrow) shows the difference in speed between the two layers.
  • Δy (yellow dimension on the right) shows the distance between the two layers.
  • du/dy = Δu ÷ Δy. It tells us how "steep" the velocity change is between the two layers.
Try this: (1) Increase only u₂ → du/dy goes up. (2) Decrease Δy (layers closer together) → du/dy goes up without changing Δu. This is why a narrow pipe produces higher wall shear stress than a wide pipe at the same speed.

Why it matters: du/dy is the "rate of deformation" of the fluid. The larger the du/dy, the faster the fluid is being kneaded, and the larger the shear stress required.

Units: velocity (m/s) ÷ distance (m) → the velocity gradient has units of s⁻¹ (per second).

6 Newton's Law of Viscosity

For a Newtonian fluid, shear stress is directly proportional to the velocity gradient.

τ ∝ du/dy Shear stress is proportional to the velocity gradient

The proportionality constant is called the dynamic viscosity, μ. Therefore:

τ = μ · du/dy Newton's Law of Viscosity

What you are seeing

A graph of τ against du/dy for a Newtonian fluid. The X-axis is velocity gradient (s⁻¹), the Y-axis is shear stress (Pa).

  • Orange straight line = linear relationship between τ and du/dy. This is the hallmark of a Newtonian fluid (water, air, ordinary oils).
  • Slope of the line = μ (dynamic viscosity). The steeper the line, the more viscous the fluid.
  • Blue dot shows your current state. It always lies exactly on the straight line.
Try this: (1) Increase μ → the line becomes steeper. (2) Increase du/dy → the blue dot moves right, τ goes up. (3) Set μ low (water) and compare with μ high (honey) — at the same du/dy, honey gives a much larger τ.

Why it matters: this graph is the "signature" of a Newtonian fluid. If the line is not straight (curved, or not through the origin), that fluid is non-Newtonian (e.g. blood, paint, ketchup).

Where the line is steeper, the fluid has a higher viscosity — the same velocity gradient produces more shear stress.

7 What Does Dynamic Viscosity Mean?

Dynamic viscosity describes how strongly a fluid resists the relative movement of its layers.

Waterflows easily Honeyflows more slowly
Simulation · Falling Through Two Fluids Same object, same gravity — different resistance
What you are seeing

Two tubes: left is water (μ ≈ 0.001 Pa·s), right is honey (μ ≈ 2 – 10 Pa·s). The same ball is dropped in both.

  • Ball in water falls quickly — water does not resist the relative motion of its layers much.
  • Ball in honey falls slowly — honey strongly resists relative motion, so it "holds" the ball.
  • Motion streaks behind the ball show the fluid being "kneaded" and forming layers moving at different speeds.
Watch this: both balls feel the same gravity. The only difference comes from the viscous resistance produced as fluid layers slide past each other.

Why it matters: viscosity determines whether a fluid is "easy" or "hard" to pump, mix, or flow. In industry, lubricating oils are chosen based on the viscosity suited to the operating temperature.

Honey has a higher viscosity than water. Therefore:

  • Higher viscosity → greater resistance to flow
  • Lower viscosity → easier flow

Connection to Newton's Law: if the velocity gradient is the same, then a higher μ gives a higher shear stress.

τ = μ · du/dy

What you are seeing

A bar chart comparing τ for three fluids at the same du/dy (controlled by the slider). The y-axis is fixed (0 – 2100 Pa) so that changes are visible.

  • Water (μ = 0.001 Pa·s) — a tiny sliver, almost invisible at the bottom.
  • Oil (μ = 0.1 Pa·s) — a low bar that grows slowly as du/dy increases.
  • Honey (μ = 5 Pa·s) — a tall bar that grows dramatically as du/dy increases.
Watch this: as you move the du/dy slider from 10 → 400 s⁻¹, the honey bar visibly grows from a small stub to nearly full height. The oil bar also grows, but only a little. The water bar stays almost flat. This is visual proof that τ = μ · du/dy — each fluid's τ grows in proportion to its own μ.

Why it matters: in pipe system design, you must know how much pressure is needed to move a fluid. Viscous fluids require much higher pressure to achieve the same du/dy — which is why pumping honey needs a far more powerful pump than water.

Higher viscosity → higher shear stress for the same velocity gradient.

Lower viscosity → lower shear stress for the same velocity gradient.

This is why pushing honey through a pipe needs far more force than pushing water through the same pipe at the same speed.

9 Shear Stress Between Two Plates

Consider a fluid between two parallel plates. The upper plate moves with velocity U; the lower plate is stationary.

Because of the no-slip condition, the fluid in contact with each plate has approximately the same velocity as the plate:

Bottom plateu ≈ 0 Top plateu ≈ U
Simulation · τ = μU/h Change plate speed, gap height and viscosity
du/dy = U/h
250 s⁻¹
τ = μU/h
25.0 Pa
What you are seeing

This is a special case of Couette flow where the velocity profile is linear (arrows grow steadily from bottom to top).

  • U = top plate speed. If U = 0, there is no motion at all.
  • h = gap between the plates. The smaller h is, the "steeper" the velocity profile.
  • μ = fluid viscosity.
  • du/dy = U/h because the profile is linear. This means a constant gradient across the whole gap.
Experiment: (1) Reduce h (e.g. 1 mm) → du/dy goes up → τ goes up. (2) Increase μ → τ goes up even though U and h are unchanged. (3) Set μ = 0 (superfluid) → τ = 0 even with motion — proof that shear stress depends on viscosity, not merely on motion.

Why it matters: the formula τ = μU/h is used in the design of journal bearings, viscometers, and lubrication systems. Engineers control h to obtain the desired τ.

τ = μ · U / h For a linear velocity profile between parallel plates

Small gap h → larger velocity gradient → higher shear stress.

Large gap h → smaller velocity gradient → lower shear stress.

11 Common Misconception

Don't say: "Shear stress only occurs when the fluid is moving."

This is too simplistic. The important factor is relative motion — the velocity gradient.

A fluid can be moving as a whole with a uniform velocity. If every layer moves at the same velocity, then du/dy = 0 and there is no viscous shear stress.

Simulation · Uniform Flow vs Shear Flow Top: everything moves together. Bottom: layers slide past each other.
What you are seeing

Two panels compared side by side:

  • Top panel (green) — UNIFORM FLOW: all layers move at the same speed. Arrows are equal length. Particles stay aligned with each other.
  • Bottom panel (orange) — SHEAR FLOW: the top layer moves faster than the bottom layer. Arrows differ in length. Particles shift relative to each other.
Watch this: in the top panel, even though the fluid is moving, there is no viscous shear stress because there is no relative motion between layers (du/dy = 0). In the bottom panel, the fluid experiences shear stress because layers slide past each other (du/dy > 0).

Why it matters: this is the most common student misconception. Shear stress is not about "there is motion" — it is about differences in speed between layers. A fluid moving uniformly does not experience viscous shear stress, even at high speed.

Cars on a highway — an analogy

Imagine you are driving on a straight highway. All cars travel at the same velocity, say 100 km/h. Relative to each other, the cars are not moving past one another.

Same velocity → no relative motion → no velocity difference between adjacent layers → velocity gradient = 0.

du/dy = 0 → τ = μ · 0 = 0 No velocity gradient → no viscous shear stress

Important clarification: the car analogy is only an analogy for relative motion. Cars are not a fluid — tyre–road friction and air resistance can still produce forces.

The real fluid example is a whole block of fluid moving together at 5 m/s, with every layer at 5 m/s: no velocity gradient → no shear stress.
