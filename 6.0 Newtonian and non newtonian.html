Newtonian
& Non-Newtonian
Fluids

Some fluids keep a constant viscosity no matter how fast they are sheared. Others change — getting thinner, thicker, or refusing to move at all until you push hard enough.

Newtonian → constant μ  ·  Non-Newtonian → changing μapp

1What is a Newtonian Fluid?

A Newtonian fluid is a fluid where the shear stress is directly proportional to the rate of deformation.

τ = μ · (du / dy) τ = shear stress · μ = dynamic viscosity · du/dy = shear rate
Simulation · Simple Shear Between Two Plates Slide to change the top plate speed
What this simulation shows A fluid is trapped between two horizontal plates. The bottom plate is fixed, and the top plate slides sideways at a speed U. The blue arrows show the fluid's velocity at different depths: it is zero at the bottom and maximum at the top, giving a straight-line velocity profile. This linear profile is exactly what Newton's law of viscosity describes — the velocity gradient du/dy is constant across the gap. Drag the slider to increase U: the arrows lengthen, meaning the shear rate increases, and the shear stress rises in direct proportion. The viscosity itself does not change — that is the signature of a Newtonian fluid.

The important idea: for a Newtonian fluid, viscosity stays constant at a given temperature and pressure.

If the shear rate increases, the shear stress increases proportionally.

2Water — The Everyday Newtonian

Consider water flowing from a tap.

Open the tap slowly → water flows easily. Open it further → the water flows faster. But the viscosity of water does not significantly change just because the flow rate increased.

3Newton's Law of Viscosity

The word Newtonian comes from the relationship described by Newton's law of viscosity:

τ = μ · γ̇ where γ̇ = du/dy is the shear rate

What this simulation shows This graph plots shear stress τ (vertical axis) against shear rate γ̇ (horizontal axis). The blue line is the equation τ = μ γ̇ — a straight line through the origin. Drag the slider and a point moves along the line, with dashed guides showing the current τ and γ̇ values. The orange triangle highlights the slope of the line, which equals the dynamic viscosity μ. No matter where you move the point, the slope never changes. This constant slope — a constant viscosity — is precisely what makes a fluid Newtonian.

4What is a Non-Newtonian Fluid?

A non-Newtonian fluid is a fluid where the relationship between shear stress and shear rate is not simply linear with a constant viscosity.

μ ≠ constant under changing shear conditions
Simulation · Newtonian vs Non-Newtonian Toggle the fluid type and compare the curves
What this simulation shows The same τ-vs-γ̇ graph is drawn for two different fluids. Switch to Newtonian and you see a straight line: shear stress is directly proportional to shear rate. Switch to Non-Newtonian and the curve bends — a nonlinear relationship. The dashed blue line stays behind as a reference showing what a Newtonian fluid would do at the same shear rates. The moving dot lets you see how the two fluids diverge more and more as the shear rate rises. The curved shape means the apparent viscosity is changing, which is the defining feature of non-Newtonian behaviour.

The important idea: a non-Newtonian fluid does not have one constant viscosity for all shear rates. Its apparent viscosity can change.

Ketchup is a shear-thinning fluid. Its apparent viscosity changes with the applied shear.

Other shear-thinning examples: paint, some polymer solutions, blood.

7What is Shear-Thinning?

Some non-Newtonian fluids become less viscous when the shear rate increases.

Shear rate ↑ → Apparent viscosity ↓ shear-thinning
Simulation · Viscosity vs Shear Rate Shear-thinning — the faster you shear, the easier it flows
What this simulation shows This graph plots apparent viscosity μ_app (vertical axis) against shear rate γ̇ (horizontal axis) for a shear-thinning fluid. The orange curve falls steeply at first and then levels off. An animated point glides along the curve so you can watch the viscosity drop in real time. At low shear rates, μ_app is high — the fluid is thick and resistant. At high shear rates, μ_app is low — the fluid flows almost like water. This is exactly why shaking a ketchup bottle makes it pour: you have moved to the right-hand side of this curve.

In simple words: the more the fluid is sheared, the easier it flows.

Real-life examples: ketchup, paint, some polymer solutions, blood.

8What is Shear-Thickening?

Some non-Newtonian fluids behave in the opposite way — they become more resistant to flow when the shear rate increases.

Shear rate ↑ → Apparent viscosity ↑ shear-thickening

Move slowly

The mixture behaves more like a liquid — your finger moves relatively easily.

Move quickly

Much greater resistance — the mixture becomes much harder to deform rapidly.

This is called shear-thickening behaviour.

9What is Yield Stress?

Some fluids do not start flowing immediately when a small force is applied. They require a minimum shear stress before flow begins.

τy yield stress — the minimum stress before flow begins
3D Simulation · Squeezing Toothpaste Hold SQUEEZE — press hard and long to beat the yield stress τy
PREPARING TOOTHPASTE…

BINGHAM TOOTHPASTE

Toothpaste stays solid until the squeeze stress beats its yield stress τy. You must press hard and long to reach it. When you release, the tube lifts and the paste stays on the brush.

τ = τy + μp·γ̇ μapp = τy/γ̇ + μp

What this 3D simulation shows A real 3D toothpaste tube, a toothbrush, and a Bingham-plastic rheology model — τ = τy + μp·γ̇ with τy = 120 Pa and μp = 8 Pa·s. The squeeze meter marks the yield threshold with a yellow line. While your squeeze stress stays below that line, nothing happens — the paste stays solid and the ribbon does not extrude. The moment you push the bar past τy, the paste suddenly yields and flows, extruding a ribbon of segments onto the brush. The flow curve (top) shows the classic Bingham kink: no flow below τy, then a straight line of slope μp. The log-log viscosity plot (bottom) shows apparent viscosity falling as μapp = τy/γ̇ + μp — infinite at rest, approaching μp at high shear. Release the button and the tube lifts; the paste stays on the brush. That is a yield-stress fluid.

If τ < τy → fluid does not flow significantly.

If τ > τy → fluid begins to flow.

This is why toothpaste can remain on your toothbrush instead of immediately running off like water.

10What is Thixotropy?

Some fluids change their viscosity depending on how long they are being sheared.

A thixotropic fluid becomes less viscous when it is continuously sheared.

Shearing over time → viscosity decreases thixotropy
3D Simulation · Shearing Latex Paint on a Wall Dip · slow stroke · fast stroke — watch the film level, thin, and dry
OPENING THE PAINT CAN…

LATEX PAINT — SHEAR THINNING

Dip, then paint. Slow strokes lay a thick coat on the top zone, fast on the bottom. Keep shearing and the film levels into an even, thin layer filling its own zone — but never crosses into the other stroke area. If the wet film sits idle, it begins to harden. After 3 full sessions the main dry kicks in; once cured, the film is immune.

Consider paint inside a container:

  • At rest → relatively thick.
  • When stirred → continued shearing reduces its apparent viscosity.
  • This helps the paint spread during application.

Some paints exhibit thixotropic behaviour. Viscosity decreases with time under shear.

11What is Apparent Viscosity?

For a Newtonian fluid:

μ = τ / γ̇ μ is constant at fixed temperature and pressure

For a non-Newtonian fluid, we often use:

μapp = τ / γ̇ apparent viscosity — depends on the flow condition
Simulation · Apparent Viscosity Comparison Same fluid at different shear rates — watch μapp change
What this simulation shows Three bars represent the apparent viscosity of ketchup at three different shear rates. The first bar (low γ̇) is tall and dark orange — ketchup at rest is thick and hard to pour. The middle bar (medium γ̇) is shorter and lighter. The third bar (high γ̇) is short and pale — ketchup being squeezed hard is thin and flows easily. A dashed arrow across the top emphasises the trend: as shear rate increases, apparent viscosity falls. This is why we cannot assign a single viscosity number to ketchup — its μ_app depends on the flow condition.

Why is apparent viscosity important?

Consider ketchup again:

  • At rest — high apparent viscosity (thick, won't pour).
  • During squeezing — lower apparent viscosity (flows out).

We cannot simply say "ketchup has one constant viscosity." Instead, its apparent viscosity depends on the flow condition.

Curved relationship → non-Newtonian behaviour.

Main idea to remember

Newtonian: τ = μ γ̇ — viscosity is approximately constant.

Non-Newtonian: μapp can change — flow behaviour depends on shear rate, time, or yield stress.

One sentence

Newtonian fluids have a constant viscosity under fixed conditions, while non-Newtonian fluids have an apparent viscosity that can change with the conditions of flow.
