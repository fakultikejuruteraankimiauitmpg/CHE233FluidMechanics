1What is Stress?

Stress is the internal force acting per unit area within a material.

In simple terms: when an external force is applied to a material, the material develops internal forces to oppose the applied force. The intensity of this internal force per unit area is called stress.

σ = FA σ = stress · F = force · A = area
Pa = N / m² The SI unit of stress is the pascal

2Why Do We Divide Force by Area?

Consider two surfaces experiencing the same force.

Simulation · Same force, different area The internal arrows show how concentrated the force is

Case 1 — Large area. The force is distributed over a large area. Therefore the stress is relatively small.

Case 2 — Small area. The same force acts over a smaller area. Therefore the stress is larger.

Same force + smaller area → greater stress
Same force + larger area → smaller stress This is exactly why we divide by area

A force alone does not tell you how severely a material is loaded. A 500 N force spread over 5 m² is gentle; the same 500 N on a pinhead is destructive.

3Two Components of Stress

Stress can be separated according to the direction in which the force acts relative to the surface. There are two important types:

1 · Normal stress 2 · Shear stress

Normal stress

Acts perpendicular to a surface. "Normal" here means perpendicular — not "usual" or "ordinary". It can produce compression (pushed) or tension (pulled).

Shear stress

Acts tangentially or parallel to a surface. It tends to make adjacent layers slide relative to each other.

Normal stress → ⊥ to surface   |   Shear stress → ∥ to surface Symbols commonly used: σ (normal) and τ (shear)

The next two pages take each one in turn — then we compare them directly.

4Normal Stress

Normal stress acts perpendicular to a surface — the force is acting at 90° to the surface.

Simulation · Compression vs Tension Switch modes and watch the material respond

Compression — the material is pushed. It shortens in the direction of the force.

Tension — the material is pulled. It lengthens in the direction of the force.

Either way, the force stays perpendicular to the surface. That is what makes it a normal stress.

5Shear Stress

Shear stress acts tangentially or parallel to a surface — the force is acting along the surface, not into it.

Simulation · Layers sliding The top portion moves relative to the bottom portion

Shear stress tends to make adjacent layers slide relative to each other. This produces shear deformation.

τ = FA τ = shear stress · F = shear force · A = area · unit: Pa = N/m²

A simple way to visualise it: normal stress pushes into the surface; shear stress drags along it.

7What Happens in a Fluid at Rest?

This is particularly important in fluid mechanics.

A fluid at rest cannot sustain a shear stress.

Why? Suppose we try to apply a shear stress: the fluid layers begin to move relative to one another. Therefore the fluid is no longer at rest.

Simulation · Try to shear a fluid at rest Toggle the shear and watch what the fluid does
Fluid at rest → no shear stress The moment it can slide, it is not at rest any more

However — a fluid at rest can have normal stress. And that normal stress has a special name.

8Normal Stress in a Fluid at Rest = Pressure

For a fluid at rest, the normal stress is called pressure.

p = FA p = pressure · F = normal force · A = area

Pressure acts perpendicular to the surface. For example, water in a tank pushes against the tank walls:

Stress
Stress is the internal force per unit area developed within a material when an external force is applied.
σ = F / A   unit: Pa = N/m²
Normal Stress
Stress that acts perpendicular to a surface. It produces compression or tension. Symbol: σ.
Shear Stress
Stress that acts tangentially or parallel to a surface, tending to make adjacent layers slide. Symbol: τ, and τ = F / A.
Fluid at Rest
A fluid at rest cannot sustain shear stress. It can, however, have normal stress — and that normal stress is called pressure.
